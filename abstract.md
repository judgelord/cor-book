This book advances and measures the concept of ***interbranch representation***---how Members of Congress work directly with federal agencies on behalf of various constituencies. Interbranch representation includes casework (constituency service) on behalf of individuals, nonprofits, and businesses, as well as oversight and engaging in executive-branch policymaking and implementation. While many members of the public---and certainly the punditry---are aware of Congress’s legislative woes, fewer are aware of the other half of the work that congressional offices do behind the scenes to help constituents and shape executive-branch policymaking.  We find that, collectively, members of Congress make over a hundred thousand requests a year directly to executive branch officials. At a time when policymaking as well as implementation is concentrated in the executive branch, the ombudsman role of congressional offices is a key facet of representation, and most congressional offices invest as much or more in interbranch work as they do in legislative work.

There are, however, massive differences in how much interbranch representation congressional offices provide. To explain this variation, we offer a framework with three types of explanations, those rooted in constituent demand, legislative office capacity, and strategic incentives. Rather than constituent demand and capacity constraints presenting trade-offs among representing different constituencies, we find higher-capacity congressional offices doing more of all types of interbranch representation. Differing levels of constituent demand matter, but most of the variation is due to different legislator capacity, particularly experience in office and committee positions that grant legislators access to additional information and staff. The main form of strategic behavior we find---legislators working much more with agencies perceived as being ideologically aligned---is consistent with a view of agencies and legislators as policy-making-and-implementing coalitions. <!--, but this behavior does not explain inequality.--> This does not explain representation of business interests, which has different drivers than other types of interbranch representation. One of those drivers is partisanship, with moderate Republicans doing substantially more for business, while more right-wing legislators provide less interbranch representation of all kinds, including less work on behalf of business. We find mixed evidence for other types of strategic behavior, such as reelection-motivated constituency service and interbranch work as a substitute for legislating when one's party is out of power.

In sum, partisanship and ideology affect choices about which agencies legislators contact and on whose behalf. But variation in interbranch representation is mostly a story of the capacity of legislators, their offices, and committees, with varying levels of constituent demand often a secondary cause. We argue that a focus on interbranch representation adds important nuance to modern narratives about a broken Congress. While Congress may be broken in many ways, one underappreciated way in which Congress still works is through interbranch representation (at least for some districts). But American citizens, just like political scientists and journalists, haven’t been paying attention to the large amount of work Congress has been doing to advocate on their behalf.
